ER Visit Cost With Insurance: What You Actually Pay

Emergency room bills are confusing by the design of the system, not because you missed something. The good news is that an ER bill breaks into a few knowable parts, your share with insurance follows a formula you can estimate, and emergency visits carry the strongest billing protections in the law. This guide covers what an ER visit costs, why the bill runs so high, and how to check and lower it.

What an ER visit costs with insurance and without

There is no single price for an ER visit. The total depends on how serious the visit was, what tests and imaging were done, whether you were admitted, and where you live. The Health Care Cost Institute found that in 2021 the average emergency room visit, counting all services, cost about $2,909, and that ER prices have climbed sharply over the past decade. An earlier 2019 analysis of large employer claims by the Peterson KFF Health System Tracker put the average total near $2,453, with the average insured patient paying roughly $646 out of pocket. These are just averages, but have most likely risen in the last few years.

With insurance

With insurance, your bill is not the sticker total. It is your share of it, and that share is set by your plan. Two numbers drive it. Your deductible is the amount you pay before the plan starts sharing costs, so if you have not met it, an ER visit often lands squarely against it. After the deductible, coinsurance is the percentage of the remaining allowed amount you keep paying, commonly around 20 percent, until you reach your out of pocket maximum, which is the yearly ceiling on what you can be made to pay. Some plans use a flat emergency room copay instead of coinsurance. The estimator below turns these numbers into an estimate of your share.

Without insurance

Without insurance, you face the full charges, and they vary enormously. Estimates commonly put a straightforward ER visit somewhere in the range of about $1,000 to $2,500, with serious cases running into the tens of thousands. Treat any figure as an estimate that depends on the care. Uninsured patients are often charged more than the rates insurers pay, but you have room to push back. Ask about the cash or self pay price, a prompt pay discount for paying quickly, and the hospital's financial assistance program, any of which can bring the number down well before you agree to pay.

Why your ER bill is so high

An ER bill is high because it is really several charges stacked together, and a few of them are large by default. Knowing the pieces is the first step to spotting the ones worth questioning.

  • The facility fee. A charge for walking through the door and using the emergency department, separate from any treatment. It is legitimate and often one of the largest lines on the bill, and it rises with how the visit is coded.
  • The ER visit level. Every visit is assigned a level that sets much of the facility charge. A higher level means a higher bill, which is why it is the single most valuable thing to check, and it has its own section below.
  • Physician bills that arrive separately. The doctor who treated you often bills apart from the hospital, and a radiologist who reads a scan may bill separately too. That is why one ER trip can produce several bills over weeks.
  • Imaging and labs. X rays, CT scans, and lab tests are billed on their own lines, and hospital pricing for them is frequently far above what the same test costs elsewhere.
  • Trauma activation. If a trauma team was called before you arrived, a trauma activation fee can add thousands of dollars, and it is worth checking that a full activation actually happened.

Each of these is explained in depth, with what a fair charge looks like and how to spot an error, in our guide to how to read a hospital bill.

The ER visit level and why it matters

Every emergency visit is billed at one of five levels using the codes 99281 through 99285. The level is meant to reflect how complex the visit was, with 99281 the simplest and 99285 the most involved, and the charge climbs steeply with each step. Because the level drives so much of the facility fee, a visit coded one level too high can add hundreds of dollars for no added care.

This makes the level the highest value thing to review on an ER bill. You do not need to know the exact criteria to sense a mismatch. If you were seen briefly for something minor, sent home quickly, and the bill shows the top level, that gap is worth a question to the billing department. Level assignment is a common place for a bill to drift upward, and it is exactly the kind of thing a careful read catches. For how this pattern, known as upcoding, shows up across a hospital bill, see the upcoding section of our hospital bill guide.

Your No Surprises Act protections in the ER

The emergency room is where your billing protections are strongest. Under the federal No Surprises Act, emergency care is protected no matter which hospital you go to and whether the providers are in your network. An out of network emergency room cannot balance bill you beyond your in network cost sharing, and the waiver that can strip this protection in other settings is never valid for emergency services.

In plain terms, if you went to the ER, you should owe only your normal in network share, even if the hospital or the doctors were out of network. A surprise out of network charge from an emergency visit is likely a violation worth challenging. For how the law works, what it does not cover, and how to file a complaint, see our guide to the No Surprises Act.

How to review an ER bill before paying

Before you pay an ER bill, walk it through a short review. Most of the money at stake on an ER bill hides in a few places, and this is where you find it. Work through these steps in order.

  1. Request an itemized bill. The first statement is usually a summary with one balance. Ask for the line by line version so you can check every charge. Here is how to request an itemized hospital bill.
  2. Confirm the ER visit level matches your visit. Find the level code, 99281 through 99285, and sanity check it against how involved the visit actually was. A brief visit billed at the top level is the first thing to question.
  3. Check for separate physician bills. Expect the hospital facility charge, the ER physician, and possibly a radiologist to bill separately. Line them up so you can confirm none repeats the same service, a pattern covered in our guide to how to read a hospital bill.
  4. Compare the bill against your EOB. Match it to what your plan allowed and paid. A bill that asks for more than your explanation of benefits says you owe is a balance bill. Our guide to reading an EOB shows the number that should match.
  5. Check the facility fee. It is a legitimate charge, but a large one, and it scales with the visit level. If it looks out of step with a simple visit, ask what it covers.
  6. Verify your No Surprises Act protection if you were out of network. For an emergency visit, an out of network provider generally cannot bill you beyond your in network cost sharing, so a surprise balance is worth disputing.

How to negotiate an ER bill

If the bill is correct but still steep, or a charge will not be fixed through a dispute, negotiation is your next move, and ER bills are often negotiable. Timing helps. It is easier to work out a lower number or a plan before the account is overdue or sent to collections, so start early rather than waiting.

Call the billing office on the statement and be specific about what you are asking for. Effective requests include a discount for paying promptly, the cash or self pay rate if you are uninsured, a reduction based on financial hardship, and an interest free payment plan if you need to spread the balance. Ask the hospital about its financial assistance program as well, since a hospital based emergency visit may qualify. Get any agreement in writing before you pay, and keep notes of every call.

When the problem is a specific wrong charge rather than the overall amount, put it in writing. The process in our guide to how to dispute a medical bill applies to ER bills too, and includes a free dispute letter you can adapt. Frequently asked questions

How much does an ER visit cost with insurance?

Your share depends on your plan. A 2019 analysis of large employer claims by the Peterson KFF Health System Tracker found insured patients paid about 646 dollars out of pocket on average for an emergency visit, a dated figure that varies widely and has likely risen since. What you owe is set by your deductible, your coinsurance, and your out of pocket maximum, or a flat emergency room copay if your plan uses one.

Why is my ER bill so high?

An ER bill stacks several charges: a facility fee for using the emergency department, the assigned visit level, separate bills from the physician and any radiologist, imaging and lab charges at hospital pricing, and sometimes a trauma activation fee. The facility fee and the visit level are usually the largest pieces and the most worth checking.

How much does an ER visit cost without insurance?

It varies enormously. Estimates commonly put a straightforward visit around 1,000 to 2,500 dollars, with serious cases reaching the tens of thousands, so treat any number as an estimate that depends on the care. If you are uninsured, ask about the cash or self pay price, a prompt pay discount, and financial assistance, which can lower the bill substantially.

Can you negotiate an ER bill?

Often yes. Call the billing office and ask for a prompt pay discount, the cash or self pay rate if you are uninsured, a hardship reduction, or an interest free payment plan. It is easier to negotiate before the account is overdue, and a hospital based emergency visit may also qualify for the hospital's financial assistance program.

What is an ER facility fee?

A facility fee is a charge for using the emergency department itself, the space, staff, and equipment, separate from the treatment you received. It is legitimate and common, and it is often one of the largest lines on an ER bill. It scales with the assigned visit level, so a higher level means a higher facility fee.

Why did I get two separate bills from the ER?

Emergency care is usually billed by more than one party. The hospital bills for the facility, and the ER physician bills separately for their professional service, and a radiologist who reads a scan may send a third bill. Getting several bills for one visit is normal, but line them up to make sure none charges twice for the same thing.

Does insurance cover emergency room visits?

Yes, health plans cover emergency care, but coverage does not mean free. You still owe your deductible, coinsurance, and any copay up to your out of pocket maximum. Under the No Surprises Act, emergency care is also protected from surprise out of network balance billing, so you should owe only your in network share.

What happens if you cannot pay an ER bill?

Do not ignore it, but know you have options. Ask for a payment plan, apply for the hospital's financial assistance, and dispute any errors first so you are only dealing with an accurate amount. An unpaid bill can eventually go to collections, but you keep the right to dispute a wrong charge even then.

What is an ER visit level and how does it affect the bill?

Emergency visits are billed at one of five levels using the codes 99281 through 99285, from least to most complex, and the charge rises sharply with each level. Because the level sets much of the facility fee, a visit coded higher than the care warranted inflates the bill, which is why checking the level is the highest value review on an ER bill.

Do I have to pay an out of network ER bill?

For an emergency, you generally owe only your in network cost sharing, even if the hospital or providers were out of network. The No Surprises Act protects emergency care from surprise out of network balance billing, and the waiver that can remove that protection elsewhere is never valid for emergency services. A surprise balance is worth challenging.
