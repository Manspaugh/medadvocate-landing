My Medical Bill Went to Collections in Texas: What Do I Do Now?

Updated November 2026

If a medical bill went to collections in Texas, do not pay it yet. Send the collector a written dispute within the 30 day window on its validation notice, which makes it pause collection of the disputed amount until it verifies the debt, as the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains. Then check whether the facility ever sent you the itemized bill that Texas law has required since September 2023 before any debt collection could start. Texas also gives you breathing room: a collector cannot garnish your wages for a consumer debt, according to the Texas Attorney General, and a lawsuit on a debt must be filed within four years under the Civil Practice and Remedies Code.

Check whether the provider was allowed to send the bill to collections

In Texas the first question is whether the provider was allowed to collect at all. Senate Bill 490, which took effect on September 1, 2023, added Chapter 185 to the Health and Safety Code. A licensed facility, including a hospital, that requests payment from a patient must submit with that request a written, itemized bill, no later than the 30th day after it receives a final payment from a third party such as your insurer. The bill must carry a plain language description of each service or supply, any billing code submitted to the insurer with the amounts billed to and paid by the insurer, and the amount the provider says you owe for each line.

The clause that matters once a bill is in collections is the enforcement clause. Under the same chapter, a health care provider may not pursue debt collection against a patient for a service or supply unless it has complied with the itemized bill requirement. If you never received an itemized bill, or got only a lump sum, write to both the facility and the collector, request the itemized bill the law requires, and ask the collector to stop until the provider has complied. A request for an itemized hospital bill is also the single most useful document for finding the errors that make bills larger than they should be.

House Bill 216, which took effect on September 1, 2025, tightened delivery. A provider may issue the itemized bill through a patient portal, by mail, or as a hard copy you pick up, but one that uses a portal must check whether you actually have an active profile there, and if you do not, or it cannot tell, it has to mail, email, or hand you a copy by the method you chose. You can ask for the bill again through that method for as long as the provider has to keep it.

Good to know

Chapter 185 applies to licensed facilities such as hospitals and does not cover a federally qualified health center. A bill from a physician's office may fall outside it, but every other protection on this page applies to any medical debt. This page is general information and is not legal advice.

Send the collector a written dispute and ask it to validate the medical debt

A collection agency must give you a validation notice in its first communication or within five days of it, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau lists what it must contain: a statement that it comes from a debt collector, the name of the creditor, the account number if there is one, an itemization of the current amount showing interest, fees, payments, and credits since a set date, and an end date for a 30 day period in which you can dispute the debt. If you dispute in writing inside that window, or ask for the name and address of the original creditor, the collector must pause collecting the amount you disputed until it responds with verification.

Texas layers its own rule on top. Under Section 392.202 of the Texas Finance Code, once you dispute the accuracy of an item in writing the collector must make a written record of the dispute, stop collecting until its investigation determines the accurate amount, and within 30 days send you a written statement that denies the inaccuracy, admits it, or says it has not had enough time to investigate. If it admits the error it has to correct the item within five business days and notify everyone it already reported the wrong information to, and if it says it has not had time, it has to change the item the way you asked while it looks. The Texas Attorney General recommends sending the dispute letter by certified mail with a return receipt, because a phone call does not trigger any of this.

"I am writing to dispute the debt you are attempting to collect under account number [number]. I request validation of this debt, including an itemized statement of every charge, the name and address of the original creditor, and the date of service. I also request confirmation that the provider issued the itemized bill required by Chapter 185 of the Texas Health and Safety Code before this account was placed for collection. Please stop collection activity until you have responded in writing."

Keep a copy of the letter and the receipt. If the collector cannot produce an itemized breakdown that holds up, the full guide to disputing a medical bill walks through the letter to send next.

Review the charges before you agree to anything

A bill does not become correct because a collector is holding it. Put the original itemized bill, your explanation of benefits, and the collector's itemization side by side and look for charges billed twice, a visit level higher than the care you received, and insurance payments or adjustments that never reached the balance. Our guide on how to read a hospital bill explains what each code and section means, and the explanation of benefits guide shows how to match the insurer's payment to the provider's charge.

Texans with state regulated insurance also have a surprise billing law that collectors rarely mention. The Texas Department of Insurance says the law bans surprise bills in emergencies, for air ambulance services, for care at an in network hospital, and for labs and imaging ordered by an in network doctor, though you can still be billed for your copay, coinsurance, or deductible. A state regulated plan shows the letters DOI or TDI on the ID card, and the department notes that the Texas law does not reach self funded employer plans or Medicare, which fall under federal rules instead. If the balance in collections is the gap between an out of network charge and what your plan paid for emergency care, read the guide on out of network balance billing before you pay a dollar of it, and call the department at 800-252-3439.

What a Texas debt collector cannot do

The Texas Debt Collection Act, Chapter 392 of the Finance Code, applies to anyone collecting a consumer debt, which the Office of Consumer Credit Commissioner contrasts with the federal act, which reaches only professional collection agencies and collection attorneys. Before a third party collector may collect in Texas it must obtain a surety bond in the amount of $10,000 and file a copy with the secretary of state. The statute then lists the tactics that are off limits.

  • Threats of arrest or seizure A collector may not threaten to have you arrested for nonpayment, or threaten to seize, repossess, or sell your property, without proper court proceedings.
  • False accusations Accusing you, or threatening to accuse you, of fraud or another crime over an unpaid bill is prohibited.
  • Misrepresenting the debt A collector may not misrepresent the character, extent, or amount of the debt, or its status in a court proceeding, may not pretend to be an attorney or a government agency, and may not send papers made to look like court documents.
  • Unauthorized fees Collecting interest, fees, or charges that your agreement with the provider does not expressly authorize, or that the law does not allow, is an unfair means of collection.

Those rules have teeth. Section 392.403 of the same chapter lets you sue for an injunction and actual damages, and for violations of the bond requirement, the file correction duty, or the rule against telling others you are refusing to pay a disputed debt, you are entitled to not less than $100 for each violation. A violation is also a deceptive trade practice under Texas law. The federal act adds its own limits, and the Office of Consumer Credit Commissioner summarizes them: no calls before 8 in the morning or after 9 at night unless you agree, and a certified letter telling the collector to stop contacting you must be honored.

Can a medical collector garnish wages or take your home in Texas

This is the fear behind most panicked searches, and in Texas the answer is reassuring. The Texas Attorney General states that debt collectors cannot garnish wages for repayment of consumer debt, and that wages may be garnished only to pay debts related to court ordered child support, back taxes, and defaulted student loans. A medical bill is consumer debt. The same page says that a residence declared as a homestead cannot be taken to pay a debt, with exceptions for the mortgage itself, home improvement liens, home equity loans, and certain taxes. The Attorney General treats a collector's threat to take your home or garnish your wages as a sign of a debt collection scam worth reporting.

Can you be sued for a medical bill in Texas and for how long

Yes, a provider or collector can sue, and the deadline is the one number every Texan with an old bill should know. Section 16.004 of the Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code requires a suit on a debt to be brought not later than four years after the day the cause of action accrues. Smaller claims go to justice court, which the Government Code gives jurisdiction over civil matters where the amount in controversy is not more than $20,000, exclusive of interest. If you are served, read the citation for the answer deadline and file a written answer with the court by that date. Ignoring the papers is how a disputed bill turns into a default judgment for the full amount.

Old debt that has been sold is handled by a separate Texas rule. Section 392.307 of the Finance Code bars a debt buyer from suing or starting arbitration after the four year period has run, and it says a payment, a reaffirmation, or any other activity does not revive the claim. A debt buyer collecting a time barred debt must also say so in its first written communication, in a conspicuous notice that begins with the words "THE LAW LIMITS HOW LONG YOU CAN BE SUED ON A DEBT." A letter that carries that notice is the collector telling you it cannot sue.

How a Texas medical collection affects your credit

For a Texan, what shows up on a credit report is governed by the policies the three nationwide bureaus adopted, plus whatever federal rule is in force. A federal rule that would have removed all medical debt from credit reports was finalized in January 2025, but the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau records that on July 11, 2025 the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas vacated it, so it is not in force. The bureau policies below are what remain.

How the three nationwide credit bureaus treat medical collections, as described by Experian in October 2025.
Situation What happens on your credit report
Medical collection under $500 Not reported at all, under a change the bureaus made in 2023, and no effect on your score.
First 365 days after the bill becomes delinquent A grace period. The collection account is not added to your report if you pay inside that window.
Unpaid collection over $500 after 365 days Can appear on your report, and can stay for seven years from the date the account became delinquent.
Collection over $500 that you pay Removed from your report once it is paid.
When a provider typically sends a bill to collections Usually once payment is 60 to 120 days past due, according to Experian.

Source: Experian, How Does Medical Debt Affect Your Credit Score, updated October 2025.

The practical takeaway is that the 365 day window and the $500 floor give you time to dispute and correct a Texas bill before it touches your score, and that paying a disputed balance only to protect your credit is rarely necessary in the first year. Our guides on whether medical bills affect your credit and on the collections timeline cover the reporting rules in more depth.

Paying, negotiating, or settling a Texas medical collection

Once the collector has validated the debt and you have checked the itemized bill, you can pay what is accurate, negotiate it down, or set up a plan. Start with the provider rather than the collector when you can, because the hospital often still controls the account and can pull it back, apply financial assistance, or correct a coding error that the agency cannot. A balance already in collections does not stop you from applying for help, and the guide on medical bill forgiveness explains how those applications work.

If you negotiate with the collector, get the agreement in writing before any money moves, stating that the payment resolves the account in full. The guide on how to negotiate a hospital bill has the specific asks that tend to work. Whatever you decide, do not accept fees or interest that your original agreement with the provider never authorized.

Where to complain about a medical debt collector in Texas

Sending a complaint to the right agency matters. The Office of Consumer Credit Commissioner handles complaints about the lenders it licenses through its helpline at 800-538-1579, has no authority over third party collection agencies, and points those complaints to the Texas Attorney General's Consumer Protection Helpline at 800-621-0508. The Attorney General asks you to file a complaint immediately if a collector threatens your home or wages. For insurance problems behind the balance, the Texas Department of Insurance takes questions at 800-252-3439, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau accepts debt collection complaints online.

Frequently asked questions

How long before a medical bill goes to collections in Texas?

There is no deadline in Texas law. Experian reports that providers typically send a bill to collections once payment is 60 to 120 days past due, though many wait longer. Since September 2023, a licensed Texas facility may not pursue debt collection until it has issued the itemized bill required by Chapter 185 of the Health and Safety Code.

What is the statute of limitations on medical debt in Texas?

Four years. Section 16.004 of the Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code requires a suit on a debt to be brought within four years after the cause of action accrues. A debt buyer that waits longer is barred from suing by Section 392.307 of the Finance Code, and a payment does not restart the clock.

Can a medical debt collector garnish my wages in Texas?

No. The Texas Attorney General states that debt collectors cannot garnish wages for repayment of consumer debt, and that wages may be garnished only for court ordered child support, back taxes, and defaulted student loans. A medical bill is consumer debt.

Can I be arrested or jailed for an unpaid medical bill in Texas?

The Texas Debt Collection Act bars a collector from threatening you with arrest for nonpayment without proper court proceedings and from falsely accusing you of fraud or another crime over an unpaid bill. The Attorney General treats threats of that kind as a sign of a debt collection scam worth reporting.

Does the hospital have to send me an itemized bill before collections in Texas?

Yes, if it is a licensed facility such as a hospital. Senate Bill 490 requires the facility to submit a written itemized bill with any request for payment, no later than 30 days after it receives final payment from your insurer, and it may not pursue debt collection unless it has done so. House Bill 216, effective September 2025, adds rules for how the bill is delivered.

Can a medical bill under $500 go on my credit report in Texas?

Under the policies the three nationwide credit bureaus adopted in 2023, medical collections under $500 are not reported at all. A larger unpaid medical collection can appear only after a 365 day grace period from the delinquency date, and a paid medical collection is removed.

Should I pay the collection agency or the hospital?

Contact the hospital first. In many cases it still controls the account, can correct coding errors, and can apply financial assistance that the collector cannot. If you do pay the collector, get the settlement terms in writing first, including a statement that the payment resolves the account in full.

What should I do if a Texas collector sues me over a medical bill?

Do not ignore the citation. Read it for the answer deadline, file a written answer with the court by that date, and raise any dispute about the charges, the missing itemized bill, or the four year limitations period. Justice courts in Texas hear civil claims of up to $20,000 exclusive of interest, which covers many medical bills.

Where do I report a debt collector in Texas?

Third party collector complaints go to the Texas Attorney General's Consumer Protection Helpline at 800-621-0508. The Office of Consumer Credit Commissioner, at 800-538-1579, handles only the lenders it licenses. You can also submit a complaint to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau online, and insurance questions go to the Texas Department of Insurance at 800-252-3439.
