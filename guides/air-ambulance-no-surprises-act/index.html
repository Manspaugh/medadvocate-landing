Air Ambulance Bills and the No Surprises Act

Updated November 2026

If you have insurance and an out of network air ambulance flew you, the provider generally cannot bill you for the difference between its charge and what your plan paid. You owe only the deductible, copayment, or coinsurance you would have owed for an in network flight. That protection came from the No Surprises Act and has applied since January 1, 2022 (CMS). It covers both helicopters and fixed wing aircraft (CMS overview of key consumer protections), and unlike some other parts of the law, an air ambulance company is never allowed to ask you to sign it away (CMS decision tree).

What the law does for an air ambulance bill

The No Surprises Act treats an out of network air ambulance flight the way it treats an emergency room visit. Your plan has to process the claim as though the provider were in network, your share is calculated from that in network amount, and the provider is barred from sending you a bill for the rest. The gap between what the air ambulance company charged and what your plan was willing to pay is no longer your problem to settle, which is the whole point of the rule (CMS overview of key consumer protections).

Two details in that rule matter more than people expect. The first is that the protection does not depend on your plan having an air ambulance company in its network. If your plan has no in network air ambulance at all, the cap on your share still applies (CMS overview). The second is that whatever you do pay must be counted toward your in network deductible and your in network out of pocket maximum, the same way a payment to an in network provider would be (45 CFR 149.130).

What the law does not do is make the flight free. You still owe your plan's normal cost sharing, and on a high deductible plan that can still be a large number. The protection caps your exposure at the in network share; it does not erase it. If the figure on your bill is larger than the share your plan documents, that is the signal something is wrong, and the rest of this guide is about what to do then.

Why air is covered when ground is not

The most confusing thing about ambulance billing is that the federal protection covers the aircraft and not the van. People who have read about the No Surprises Act often assume it covers any ambulance, then get a ground ambulance balance bill and conclude the law failed them. It did not fail; ground transport was deliberately left out. Our guide to ambulance bills covers the ground side, where negotiation rather than federal law is usually your strongest tool.

The reason for the split is a law from decades earlier. The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978 bars states from regulating the prices charged by air carriers, and an air ambulance counts as an air carrier. That single fact meant states could not write their own air ambulance billing protections even where they wanted to, so a federal rule was the only instrument available (an HHS issue brief on air ambulance use). Ground ambulances sit outside that restriction, and the No Surprises Act leaves them out, so whether a ground ambulance can balance bill you depends on where you live (CMS overview).

The protection no one can ask you to sign away

Parts of the No Surprises Act can be waived. For some non emergency care from an out of network provider at an in network facility, you can be handed a notice and consent form, and if you sign it you give up the billing protection for that service (CMS overview). It is the part of the law that can cost you money, because the form arrives in a stack of admission paperwork and looks procedural.

Air ambulance is different. An air ambulance provider may never ask you to consent to waive these protections, and there is no version of the form that works for a flight. So if an air ambulance company presents you with anything that reads like a waiver, a consent to be billed at its own rates, or an agreement to accept financial responsibility for charges above your plan's payment, that document does not do what it appears to do (CMS decision tree).

Signing a form you were handed on a helipad or in a transfer bay does not cancel your air ambulance protection. Keep the document, note the date, and treat it as evidence that the provider was trying to bill outside the rules rather than as a bill you now owe.

When the protection does not apply

The cap is not universal, and knowing its edges tells you whether your own bill is covered. The protection runs through your health plan, so it depends on you having one and on that plan covering the kind of transport you received (CMS overview).

  • Your plan does not cover that type of flight. A plan that covers air ambulance only for emergencies is not required to cover a non emergency transfer between hospitals, and if it does not cover it, there is no in network amount to cap your share against.
  • You are uninsured or paying cash. The protection is built on the relationship between a provider and a plan. Without a plan, you rely instead on a good faith estimate, an itemized bill, and negotiation.
  • You are on Medicare or Medicaid. You are already protected, and have been for longer. Air ambulance providers were barred from balance billing Medicare and Medicaid patients before the No Surprises Act took effect, so the new rule changed nothing for you (HHS ASPE).
  • It was a ground ambulance. Federal protection does not reach it. State or municipal rules may, and our guide to out of network balance billing explains how to tell which apply to you.
Good to know

The distinction that decides most cases is not whether the flight was an emergency, but whether your plan covers the type of transport you received. Read the air ambulance line in your plan documents before you accept that a bill is legitimate.

What an air ambulance flight is billed at

Air ambulance charges are the reason this protection exists. They are not in the same range as other transport bills, and if nothing caps your share, the number that lands can be life altering. Flights are also rare enough that almost nobody has seen one before their own arrives: roughly one transport a year for every four thousand privately insured people, and about one in three hundred and fifty Medicare beneficiaries (HHS ASPE).

You almost never choose the company that flies you. You are unconscious, or you are being moved between hospitals by people making a clinical decision, and nobody checks your insurance network first. That is the same structural problem behind an out of network emergency room bill: the charge is set by a provider you had no opportunity to select.

What to do if you were billed anyway

Protected does not mean the wrong bill never arrives. It arrives often enough that you should treat a large air ambulance balance as a billing error to be corrected rather than a debt to be paid. Work in this order.

  1. Find your explanation of benefits. It states what your plan paid and what you owe. If the provider's bill asks for more than the amount your plan lists as your responsibility, you have found the discrepancy that matters. Our guide to reading an explanation of benefits walks through the lines that count.
  2. Call your plan first, not the provider. Ask whether the claim was processed under the No Surprises Act air ambulance provisions. Sometimes it was not, and the plan can reprocess it, which fixes the bill without you arguing with the air ambulance company at all.
  3. Then tell the provider in writing. State that the transport is covered by the federal air ambulance protection, that you are responsible only for your in network cost sharing, and that you are disputing the balance. Keep it short and factual, and send it so that you have a record.
  4. Appeal if your plan denied or underpaid the claim. A denial is not the end of the process, and an appeal is often what moves a claim into the right category.
  5. Report it to the No Surprises Help Desk. The help desk reviews whether the provider or the plan followed the surprise billing rules and can refer the complaint to the federal or state enforcement authority that covers it. You can call 1-800-985-3059 or submit a complaint online.

Have your bill, your insurance card, and your explanation of benefits ready when you call, along with any waiver style document you were handed. The help desk answers billing questions and can help you in Spanish, French, Arabic, Russian, Nepali, and 350 other languages (CMS).

Filing a complaint does not pause the bill. You are still responsible for the amount your explanation of benefits lists as yours, so pay that part while you dispute the rest. Leaving the whole balance unpaid is what turns a billing error into a collections problem.

What happens between the provider and your plan

Once you are out of the middle, the air ambulance company and your plan still have to settle on a number, and understanding that helps explain why a wrong bill reaches you in the first place. If the provider thinks the plan paid too little, it does not come to you. It enters a federal dispute process in which an independent entity picks between the two sides' offers, under deadlines set by regulation (CMS overview).

That process is between the provider and the plan. You are not a party to it, you do not file anything, and its outcome does not change what you owe. Your share was already fixed by your plan's in network cost sharing. When an air ambulance balance bill lands in your mailbox, it frequently means the provider is trying to recover through you what it should be pursuing through that process instead, which is exactly the behavior the ban was written to stop.

If you are holding a flight bill and are not sure which parts are legitimately yours, the general method applies: get the itemized bill, compare it against the explanation of benefits, and put your dispute in writing. Our guide to disputing a medical bill sets out those steps in detail, and they work the same way on an air ambulance balance as on a hospital charge.

Frequently asked questions

Does the No Surprises Act cover air ambulance bills?

Yes. Air ambulance transport is one of the three situations the No Surprises Act covers, alongside emergency care and some non emergency care at an in network facility. If you have insurance that covers the flight and the provider was out of network, you owe only your in network cost sharing and the provider cannot bill you for the rest. It applies to both helicopters and fixed wing aircraft.

Does it cover ground ambulances too?

No. Ground ambulance transport was left out of the No Surprises Act, so in much of the country a ground ambulance can still balance bill you for an out of network ride. Some states and cities have their own rules. Because federal aviation law stops states from regulating what air carriers charge, air ambulance needed a federal fix while ground ambulance did not.

Can an air ambulance company ask me to waive the protection?

No. Unlike the notice and consent process that applies to some non emergency care, an air ambulance provider may never ask you to consent to waive these protections. If you were handed a form that looks like a waiver or an agreement to pay charges above what your plan covers, it does not remove your protection. Keep it, because it is evidence.

I still got a bill for thousands of dollars. What now?

Start with your explanation of benefits and find the amount your plan says you owe. If the provider is asking for more than that, call your plan and ask whether the claim was processed under the air ambulance provisions, since a reprocessed claim often fixes the bill. Then dispute the balance with the provider in writing, and report it to the No Surprises Help Desk at 1-800-985-3059.

Does the protection mean the flight costs me nothing?

No. You still owe the deductible, copayment, or coinsurance you would have owed for an in network flight, and on a high deductible plan that can still be a significant amount. What the law removes is the balance bill, meaning the gap between the provider's charge and what your plan paid. Whatever you do pay counts toward your deductible and out of pocket maximum.

What if my plan has no in network air ambulance provider?

The protection still applies. It does not depend on your plan having contracted with an air ambulance company, which many plans have not. Your plan must still calculate your share as though the provider were in network, and the provider still cannot balance bill you.

Am I protected if I do not have insurance?

Not by this rule, because it works through the relationship between a provider and a health plan. An uninsured patient relies instead on the good faith estimate rules, on requesting an itemized bill, and on negotiating the charge directly. Medicare and Medicaid patients were already protected from air ambulance balance billing before the No Surprises Act.

Should I pay anything while I dispute the bill?

Yes, pay the amount your explanation of benefits lists as your responsibility. Filing a complaint does not pause the bill, and leaving the entire balance unpaid is what sends a billing error toward collections. Pay the part that is genuinely yours and dispute the rest in writing.
