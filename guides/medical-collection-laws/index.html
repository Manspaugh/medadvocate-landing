Medical Collection Laws: What Protects You When a Medical Bill Goes to Collections

Updated November 2026

Medical collection laws work in layers. Federally, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act requires a collection agency to send you validation information within five days of first contact and gives you a 30 day window to dispute the debt in writing, during which collection of the disputed amount must pause, according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. A nonprofit hospital must also wait at least 120 days from your first billing statement after discharge, and screen you for financial assistance, before it reports you to a credit bureau, sells the debt, or sues, under Internal Revenue Service rules for tax exempt hospitals. On credit reports, the three nationwide bureaus do not show unpaid medical collections under $500, wait a 365 day grace period before reporting larger ones, and remove medical collections once paid, as Experian explains. A growing list of states bars medical debt from credit reports entirely, and every state limits how long a collector has to sue. This guide walks through each law and how to use it.

Which medical collection laws apply to your bill

Medical collection laws come in layers, and the first job is to work out which layer you are standing on. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act is the main federal law that governs debt collection practices, according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and under it debt collectors include collection agencies, debt buyers, and lawyers. It does not generally cover collection by the original creditor or business you owed money to, which means a hospital's own billing department calling about its own bill is usually outside the federal act. Most states have debt collection laws of their own, many similar to the federal one, and some of those state laws do cover the original creditor.

On top of that act sit three more sets of rules that apply only because the debt is medical: the Internal Revenue Service's billing and collection requirements for nonprofit hospitals, the credit reporting policies the three nationwide bureaus follow for medical collections, and a growing list of state statutes that keep medical debt off credit reports altogether. This guide takes each in turn, then covers the statute of limitations, wage garnishment, and the federal surprise billing law that can make the underlying bill itself illegal. If you are still trying to work out the timeline, the companion guide on whether medical bills can be sent to collections covers how an account gets there; this page covers the rules that bind the people collecting it.

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act and the medical debt validation notice

The first thing the federal act gives you is information. A collector must provide validation information in a written notice sent as its initial communication or within five days of it, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says that notice generally must include the name of the creditor you owe, the current amount of the debt, an itemization of that amount reflecting interest, fees, payments, and credits, and an end date for a 30 day period in which you can dispute the debt.

The second thing it gives you is a pause. If you dispute the debt in writing within that 30 day window, the collector must pause collecting the amount you are disputing until it has adequately responded to your request, as the same explanation notes. For a medical bill that pause is where the mistakes come out: a charge billed twice, an insurance payment that never posted, or a balance that should have been covered by financial assistance. Put the dispute in writing, send it so you have proof of delivery, and keep a copy.

"I am writing within the validation period to dispute the debt you are attempting to collect under account number [number]. Please provide the name and address of the original creditor, an itemized statement of every charge with the date of service, and the insurance payments and adjustments applied. Please stop collection of the disputed amount until you have responded in writing."

If the itemization the collector sends does not match the hospital's own statement, request an itemized hospital bill directly from the provider and compare the two line by line. The full guide to disputing a medical bill covers what to send once you find a discrepancy, and how to escalate if the collector ignores it.

Medical debt collector call limits and harassment rules

Federal law also limits how a collector may treat you. Collectors are generally prohibited from contacting you before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m., and a collector is presumed to violate the law if it calls you about a particular debt more than seven times within a seven day period, or within seven days after having a telephone conversation with you about that debt, as the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains. Collectors also have to follow the instructions you give them about when and where you do not want to be contacted, and if they know you are not allowed to receive personal calls at work, they may not call you there.

The bureau's list of unfair, deceptive, or abusive practices reads like a catalog of the tactics people report with medical accounts:

  • Misrepresenting the debt Misstating the nature of the debt, including the amount owed. For a medical account, that includes quoting a balance that ignores an insurance payment or an adjustment.
  • Borrowed authority Falsely claiming that the person contacting you is an attorney, or that the caller represents a government agency.
  • Threats of arrest Threatening to have you arrested, or saying you will be imprisoned if you do not pay.
  • Empty threats Threatening to do things that cannot legally be done, or that the collector has no intention of doing, such as saying it will garnish your wages or seize your property when it holds no judgment.
  • Added charges Trying to collect charges in addition to the debt unless the contract or the law allows them.
  • Harassment Repetitious phone calls meant to harass, obscene or profane language, and threats of violence or harm.

Arrest deserves its own sentence because the threat comes up so often. It is a violation of the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act for a collector to have you arrested or to claim you will be arrested if you do not pay a debt, according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. The one real exception runs through a courtroom: if you are sued and do not comply with a court order, a judge may issue a warrant. That is a reason to answer any lawsuit, not a reason to fear a phone call.

Nonprofit hospital collection rules under Section 501(r)

If the bill came from a nonprofit hospital, a separate federal rule applies before any collector is involved. Section 501(r)(6) of the Internal Revenue Code requires a hospital organization to make reasonable efforts to determine whether you are eligible for assistance under its financial assistance policy before engaging in what the rule calls extraordinary collection actions, and the Internal Revenue Service lists what those actions are: selling your debt to another party, reporting adverse information about you to credit bureaus, deferring or denying medically necessary care or requiring a payment before providing it because of nonpayment, and anything that requires a legal or judicial process, such as placing a lien on your property, garnishing your wages, or commencing a civil action against you.

The timeline is specific. The regulations set a 120 day notification period and a 240 day application period, both beginning on the date of the first billing statement after discharge, and the hospital must refrain from initiating those collection actions for at least 120 days. Before it starts one, it has to give written notice that states a deadline no earlier than 30 days after the notice is provided. If you apply and are found eligible, the hospital must take all reasonably available measures to reverse any extraordinary collection action it already took, and refund what you paid above the amount you are personally responsible for as an eligible patient, unless the excess is less than $5.

In practice this means a nonprofit hospital that reported your account to a credit bureau or sold it to a debt buyer inside that window, or without screening you for assistance, may have broken the conditions of its own tax status, and saying so in writing tends to get a billing office's attention. Our guide to medical bill forgiveness explains how to find the policy and apply, and applying late is still worth it because the application period runs at least 240 days from that first statement under the same rule.

Good to know

Section 501(r) binds hospitals that hold federal tax exempt status. A for profit hospital, a physician group, or a standalone lab is not covered by it, but every other law on this page still applies to their bills. This page is general information and is not legal advice.

Medicare's collection standard for hospital bad debt

People searching for medical debt collection rules from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services usually mean one of two things: the surprise billing law covered below, or Medicare's bad debt rule. The bad debt rule governs when a hospital may ask Medicare to reimburse a deductible or coinsurance a patient never paid, and in doing so it sets a floor on how the hospital must pursue the patient first. Under 42 CFR 413.89, the provider must be able to establish that reasonable collection efforts were made, that the debt was actually uncollectible when claimed as worthless, and that sound business judgment established there was no likelihood of recovery at any time in the future.

The same regulation spells out the collection effort in days: it must last at least 120 days, and a payment received within a 120 day collection period starts a new 120 day collection period. That is one reason a hospital keeps sending statements on a Medicare balance for months before anything else happens. If you are a Medicare patient and a collector is still pursuing a coinsurance balance long after discharge, it is fair to ask the hospital in writing whether that amount has already been claimed as a Medicare bad debt.

Credit reporting laws for medical collections

For many people the rules that matter most are the ones deciding whether a medical collection ever reaches a credit report. A credit reporting company generally can report most negative information for seven years, according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, but medical collections are treated more gently than that general rule under policies Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion adopted for medical accounts specifically.

How the three nationwide credit bureaus currently treat medical collection accounts. These are bureau policies rather than statutes, so a dispute citing them goes to the bureau, not to a court.
Medical collection account Current treatment
Paid, over $500 Removed from your credit report once it is paid
Unpaid, under $500 Not shown on your credit report and does not affect your score, a change the bureaus made in 2023
Unpaid, over $500 Not reported until a 365 day grace period after the delinquency date has passed
Reported and still unpaid Can remain for seven years after the account became delinquent

Source: Experian, How Does Medical Debt Affect Your Credit Score?

A federal rule would have gone further. On January 7, 2025, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau issued a final rule under Regulation V to keep medical debt off credit reports, but on July 11, 2025, the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas vacated it at the joint request of the bureau and the plaintiffs in Cornerstone Credit Union League v. CFPB, agreeing that the rule exceeded the bureau's statutory authority, as the bureau's own rule page now records. The federal baseline today is therefore the bureau policy in the table above, plus whatever your state has added. State medical debt collection laws that go further

A growing number of states have written the credit reporting protection into statute, and most of them go further than the bureaus by barring the hospital or the collector from reporting medical debt in the first place. Whether these state laws hold up against a federal challenge is now an open question. In November 2025 the California Attorney General issued a consumer alert saying it remains illegal for medical debt to appear on a credit report in California, in response to an interpretive rule from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau claiming that federal law generally preempts state medical debt laws, a position the state rejects. If a medical collection shows up on your report in one of these states, cite the state statute in your dispute to the bureau and send a copy to your state attorney general.

Some states regulate the collection itself, not only the reporting. Texas, for example, bars a health care provider from pursuing debt collection against a patient unless it has first sent the itemized bill its law requires, under a statute that took effect September 1, 2023. Our guide to a medical bill that went to collections in Texas walks through that state's rules in full.

Statute of limitations on medical debt and wage garnishment

Every state limits how long a creditor has to sue over a debt. Most states have statutes of limitations between three and six years for debts, though some may be longer, and the period can depend on the type of debt, the state where you live, and the state law named in your agreement, according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Once the period has passed, a collector in most states can still attempt to collect, but it cannot sue or threaten to sue you. Be careful with old medical accounts, because making a partial payment or acknowledging that you owe an old debt may restart the time period.

Garnishment requires a court judgment first, and federal law caps it. For ordinary garnishments, meaning those not for support, bankruptcy, or taxes, the weekly amount may not exceed the lesser of 25 percent of your disposable earnings or the amount by which your disposable earnings exceed 30 times the federal minimum wage, and an employer may not fire you because your earnings are garnished for any one debt, per the U.S. Department of Labor. State law can protect more than that federal floor, which is another reason to look up your own state's rules before a collector's threat of garnishment moves you to pay a bill you have not checked.

When the bill itself is illegal under the No Surprises Act

Some medical debts should never have existed, and a collector does not make an illegal balance legal by buying it. Starting in 2022, the federal No Surprises Act bans surprise bills for most emergency services, bans out of network charges and balance bills for certain additional services furnished by out of network providers as part of a visit to an in network facility, and covers out of network air ambulance providers, and you cannot be charged more than in network cost sharing for these services, as the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services explain. If the balance a collector is chasing is the gap between an out of network charge and what your plan paid for emergency care, read our guide on out of network balance billing before you pay any of it.

Uninsured and self pay patients get a different protection under the same law: a good faith estimate of the cost before care, and the right to dispute a bill whose final charges are at least $400 higher than that estimate, filed within 120 days of the date on the bill, with the federal help desk at 1-800-985-3059 as the starting point, according to the same fact sheet.

How to put the medical collection laws to work

  1. Identify who is contacting you. If it is the hospital's own billing office, the federal collection act mostly does not apply, but the nonprofit hospital rules and your state's law might. If it is an agency or a debt buyer, every federal right above applies from the first call.
  2. Dispute in writing inside the validation window. Use the letter above, ask for the itemization and the original creditor, and keep proof of delivery. Collection of the disputed amount pauses while the collector responds.
  3. Ask a nonprofit hospital for its financial assistance policy. Request it in writing, note the date of your first billing statement, and apply even if the account has already moved to an agency.
  4. Pull all three credit reports. Check for a medical collection that is paid, under the bureau threshold, inside the grace period, or barred by your state's statute, and dispute it with the bureau that lists it.
  5. Match the itemization to the bill and the explanation of benefits. A collector's balance is only as accurate as the account it was handed. Compare the collector's itemization against the hospital's itemized bill and your insurer's statement before agreeing to any amount.
  6. Complain when a rule is broken. You can submit a complaint to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and contact your state attorney general's office, which the bureau points to for state specific rules. Keep a log of every call, letter, and date, because the record is what turns a complaint into a result.

None of these laws erases a bill you actually owe. What they do is slow the process down, force the collector to prove the amount, and keep an unverified balance off your credit while you check it. The checking is the part that saves money, and it starts with the itemized bill.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main federal law on medical debt collection?

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act is the main federal law governing debt collection, and it applies to collection agencies, debt buyers, and collection attorneys rather than to the hospital or clinic collecting its own bill. It requires a validation notice, gives you a window to dispute the debt in writing, limits when and how often a collector may call, and bans threats, false statements, and added charges. Nonprofit hospitals are separately bound by Internal Revenue Code Section 501(r), and most states have their own collection laws, some of which do cover the original provider.

How long does a collector have to send a validation notice for a medical debt?

A debt collector must provide validation information in its initial communication or within five days of it. The notice must name the creditor, state the current amount, itemize interest, fees, payments, and credits, and give the end date of a 30 day period in which you can dispute the debt. If you dispute in writing inside that period, the collector has to pause collecting the disputed amount until it has adequately responded.

Can a hospital send a medical bill to collections without notice?

Federal law does not set a single notice rule for every provider. A nonprofit hospital must wait at least 120 days from the first billing statement after discharge, screen you for financial assistance, and give at least 30 days written notice before it reports you to a credit bureau, sells the debt, or sues. A for profit provider follows state law and its own policy, and some states, including Texas, require an itemized bill before any collection. Once an agency holds the account, it must send validation information within five days of first contacting you.

Is there a new medical debt collection law in 2025?

The biggest change in 2025 was a rule that did not take hold. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau finalized a rule in January 2025 to remove medical debt from credit reports, and a federal court in Texas vacated it in July 2025. Several states passed their own laws instead. California's ban on medical debt in credit reports took effect January 1, 2025, Washington signed one in April 2025, and Oregon signed one later that year that takes effect in 2026.

Do medical bills under $500 go to collections?

Yes, a provider can place a medical bill of any size with a collection agency, and the collector can contact you about it. What the bureaus changed is reporting: unpaid medical collection accounts under $500 do not appear on your credit report and do not affect your credit score. The collector can still ask you to pay, and the debt can still be sued on within your state's statute of limitations.

What is the seven in seven rule for debt collectors?

Under the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's debt collection rule, a collector is presumed to violate federal law if it calls you about a particular debt more than seven times within a seven day period, or within seven days after having a telephone conversation with you about that debt. The limit applies per debt and to calls the collector places, and collectors are also generally barred from calling before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m.

How long can a medical collection stay on my credit report?

An unpaid medical collection account over $500 can remain on your credit report for seven years after the account became delinquent. It cannot be reported until a 365 day grace period after the delinquency date has passed, and once it is paid it is removed. Medical collections under $500 are not reported at all under current bureau policy, and several states bar medical debt from credit reports entirely.

What is the statute of limitations on medical debt?

It depends on your state. Most states set a statute of limitations between three and six years for debts, and some are longer. After it runs out a collector can still ask you to pay in most states, but it cannot sue or threaten to sue. Making a partial payment or acknowledging that you owe an old debt may restart the clock, so check the date of service and your state's rule before you pay anything on an old account.

Can a medical debt collector garnish my wages?

Only after suing you and winning a court judgment, and a collector that threatens garnishment without one is violating federal law. Even with a judgment, federal law limits an ordinary garnishment to the lesser of 25 percent of your disposable earnings or the amount by which those earnings exceed 30 times the federal minimum wage, and your employer cannot fire you over a garnishment for a single debt. State law may protect more.

Where do I report a medical debt collector who breaks the law?

Submit a complaint to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and contact your state attorney general's office, which enforces your state's own collection law. If a nonprofit hospital skipped the financial assistance screening or the waiting period, raise it with the hospital's billing office in writing and cite Section 501(r). Keep copies of every notice, letter, and call log, because the record is what makes a complaint stick.
